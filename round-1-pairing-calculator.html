Under the FIDE Dutch system the first round is fixed by the starting list alone, so this is calculated, not predicted. Take both numbers off the tournament's startlist.

How the first round is paired

All players start on zero, so the whole field is a single scoregroup ordered by starting number. The Dutch system splits that group in half: the top half becomes S1, the bottom half S2. The first player of S1 meets the first player of S2, the second meets the second, and so on down the list.

Field of 2n players  →  1 v n+1  ·  2 v n+2  ·  …  ·  n v 2n

With an odd number of players the lowest-ranked player receives the pairing-allocated bye, and the remaining even field is split as above. This is why a field of 81 pairs identically to a field of 80.

Why nobody can tell you your colour

Colours in round one are not derived from the ratings. The colour of the highest board is decided by a drawing of lots, and every subsequent S1 player alternates from there. So the pairing is certain but the colours are a coin flip — one that resolves every board at once.

That means there are exactly two possible colour assignments for the whole round, not two per board. Once you see any single board's colours, you know your own. The tool above gives you both branches rather than guessing.

When this will be wrong

The arithmetic is exact. The assumptions are what break:

  • The entry list changed. A late entry or a no-show shifts every starting number after it. This is the single commonest cause of a wrong answer at weekend opens, and there is no way to detect it in advance.
  • The event uses acceleration. Accelerated pairings deliberately break the halving described above, usually in large opens over the first two or three rounds.
  • Another pairing system. Dubov, Burstein and Lim are all FIDE-approved and all pair round one differently from the Dutch system.
  • Local restrictions. Many organisers ask their software to avoid pairing players from the same club or federation in the early rounds. This is not part of the FIDE system, it is rarely published, and it silently changes several boards.

If your event does any of these, treat the result as a rough guide. If it is an ordinary Dutch-paired Swiss with a settled entry list, the result should match the published pairings exactly.
