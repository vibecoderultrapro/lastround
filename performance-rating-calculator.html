How the performance rating is calculated

The Title Regulations define it in three steps, and the tool above shows each one.

Ra = the opponents' ratings, totalled and divided by the number of opponents, rounded to a whole number with .5 going up
dp = the rating difference the regulations assign to your score percentage
Rp = Ra + dp

Your score is turned into a percentage and rounded to a whole number before the table is consulted. This matters more than it looks. Six out of nine is 66.67%, which rounds to 67% and gives +125. Round it down to 66% instead and you get +117 — an eight point error, which is plenty to lose a norm on.

The adjusted rating floor

For norm purposes each title sets a minimum opponent rating: 2200 for GM, 2050 for IM, 2000 for WGM, 1850 for WIM. An opponent rated below it is counted as if they were rated at the floor.

But only one of them. Article 1.4.6c is explicit that no more than one opponent may be raised, and that where several are below the floor it is the lowest rated one that gets raised. Not the one that would help you most — the lowest. Everyone else counts at their real rating.

The practical consequence catches people out. If you have already played someone rated 1800 and your last-round opponent turns out to be unrated, the floor moves to the unrated player and the 1800 reverts to counting as 1800. A calculator that raises both to 2050 will hand you an average roughly 28 points too high across nine games.

It also means an opponent below the floor is a cliff rather than a slope. For an IM norm, a 1600 and a 2040 contribute exactly the same to your average, provided they are your lowest. Beyond that point a weaker opponent costs you nothing at all.

One weak opponent early can still quietly end a norm hunt. Because the floor is the same for everybody, a single game against a 2050 in a field of 2450s pulls your average down by around forty points over nine rounds, and no later result can undo it.

Why this disagrees with Chess-Results

Chess-Results shows a performance rating computed under the FIDE Rating Regulations. Those cap any rating difference above 400 points, and they know nothing about which title you are chasing, so they cannot apply a title's rating floor. Norms are governed by the Title Regulations, which have no 400-point rule and do have the floor.

Both numbers are correct for their own purpose. Only one of them is the number your arbiter will put on the certificate. The same set of games produces four different performance ratings depending on the title, and the figure on your player card is none of them.

Unplayed games

Games decided by forfeit or by any means other than play at the board are excluded from your score and from your opponent count. So are byes. A nine-round event where you took a bye leaves you with eight games, not nine.

There is one narrow rescue: if the shortfall was caused by a forfeit win or a pairing-allocated bye, eight games can count as a nine-game norm. A bye you asked for is neither of those, so it does not qualify, and a norm is not possible in a nine-round event. The tool says so plainly rather than quietly computing over eight games.

Ten rounds and longer

You may leave out games you won, and you may leave out games played after the norm was already achieved, provided at least nine games remain and the opponent mix still holds. There is no rule permitting any nine consecutive rounds — a first-round loss cannot be discarded.

Dropping a win sounds perverse and occasionally isn't. A win over a weak opponent can drag your opponent average down further than the extra half point lifts your percentage. The tool flags this when the full set falls short and a permitted subset does not.

What this does not check

A performance rating is not a norm. Norms also require at least half your opponents to be titled, a minimum of three from the relevant title band, limits on how many share your federation, at least two other federations represented, and a signed arbiter's certificate. None of that is checked here.

Nothing on this page will tell you that you have a norm, because nothing on this page is in a position to know.
